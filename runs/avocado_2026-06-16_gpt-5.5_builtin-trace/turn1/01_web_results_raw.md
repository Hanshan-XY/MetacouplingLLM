| `title` | From Cocaine to Avocados: Criminal Market Expansion and Violence \| International Organization |
| `url` | https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0020818325100763/type/journal_article |

**Model summary:**

> This peer-reviewed International Organization article by Chelsea Estancona and Lucía Tiscornia examines when organized criminal groups expand into licit commodity markets and when that expansion produces violence. Mexican avocados are a central case. The abstract argues that rapid increases in a good’s share of export value create opportunities for immediate profit and future market manipulation, which can lead organized criminal groups to expand territorial holdings and economic portfolios. The authors provide subnational evidence using Mexican avocado export data and address reverse causality using Google Trends data for the popularity of “avocado toast” searches. They also provide cross-national evidence using the Atlas of Economic Complexity, V-Dem, and UNODC data. The article finds that increases in a country’s share of global export value for agricultural goods are associated with more homicides, but only where organized criminal groups are present. The Cambridge page states that the Mexico-specific analysis combines municipal homicide data with avocado export-value data and criminal-presence data, and that increases in a municipality’s share of avocado export value are associated with increases in homicides where organized criminal groups are present. The study’s policy implication is that violence linked to international markets for legal goods cannot be addressed solely through drug-market or local-security lenses. For avocado sustainability research, it provides systematic evidence connecting export-market growth and criminal violence.

| `title` | Organized Crime in Michoacan: Rent-Seeking Activities in the Avocado Export Market \| Politics & Policy |
| `url` | https://produccion.siia.unam.mx/Publicaciones/ProdCientif/PublicacionFrw.aspx?id=501454 |

**Model summary:**

> This Politics & Policy article by Ruth G. Ornelas examines organized crime’s extraction of rents from Michoacán’s legal avocado export market during the 2001–2014 period. The UNAM publication record summarizes the article’s purpose as contributing to measurement of organized-crime extortion and assessing the effect of predatory activity on economic growth. The article focuses on the Knights Templar criminal organization and asks how it extracted rents from the legal avocado export market, what the estimated magnitude of those rents was, how important that revenue source was to organized crime, and what adverse economic impacts the extortions had on Michoacán’s economy. The summary emphasizes that extortion should be evaluated beyond reported crime counts because the key issue is how much money is drained from the legal economy. For sustainability research, this source is valuable because it links avocado trade to governance, local economic distortion, and criminal capture of value chains. Unlike sources centered on deforestation or water, it provides an economic-crime framework for understanding why highly profitable export crops may generate social unsustainability. It is also foundational for later reports and studies that cite organized-crime involvement in avocado production, land control, money laundering, and extortion. The geographic scope is Michoacán, and the temporal focus is 2001–2014, making it a historical source for the roots of current criminal involvement.

| `url` | https://michoacan.gob.mx/noticias/libre-de-deforestacion-el-90-del-aguacate-de-exportacion-secma/ |

**Model summary:**

> This December 2025 Michoacán state government release presents the state’s account of progress under the voluntary Pro Forest Avocado certification scheme. It reports that Alejandro Méndez López, head of Michoacán’s Environment Secretariat, said 90% of avocado exports to the United States were currently free of deforestation due to advances in the certification. The release states that 37 packing houses in Michoacán and Jalisco had obtained the compliance certification. It describes the certification requirements as obligating product to be free of deforestation recorded after January 2018, prohibiting purchase of fruit from properties affected by fires since January 2012, and excluding properties with pending environmental complaints. It also states that more than 900,000 avocado harvest checks had been conducted using the Guardián Forestal system. The release claims the voluntary certification offers full traceability, independence from producers and packers, scientific evidence, and environmental due diligence. It also says the program will be strengthened through water-footprint verification for each harvest under a future Guardián del Agua program. This source is important as the official government/industry-response perspective to deforestation concerns. It should be interpreted as a government communication rather than independent verification, but it provides specific dates, thresholds, and mechanisms for Mexico’s emerging deforestation-free avocado-export framework.

### Result 24

| Key | Value |
|---|---|
| `title` | Sustainability of the community model of avocado production in the Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve, Michoacán, México \| GeoJournal |
| `url` | https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10708-024-11195-3 |

**Model summary:**

> This 2024 GeoJournal article studies a community model of avocado production in Indigenous communities in Zitácuaro, within the eastern Michoacán Avocado Belt and the broader Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve context. It is valuable because it complicates the assumption that all avocado production in Michoacán follows the same agro-industrial pattern. The authors state that policy and scientific discourse often focuses on central Michoacán or on conventional versus organic production, overlooking diversity in governance and production models. The study characterizes and evaluates a community model embedded in local environmental governance, where avocado production is combined with forestry, milpa agriculture, livestock, and other fruit orchards. It reports that avocado revenues support community institutions such as forest monitoring and water committees, and that producers recognize the dependence of avocado on forest conservation for water supply. The article describes local rules limiting communal forest parcelization and restricting sale or rental of communal lands to outsiders; these rules help limit land-use change and external land control. It also reports average small orchard sizes of about 1.5 hectares and local workforce practices that strengthen community identity. However, the source also identifies sustainability concerns: water access is unequal and inconsistent, agrochemical use is high, and dependence on technical advisers and agrochemical stores can undermine safer practices. This source offers evidence of possible sustainability pathways based on community governance, diversification, forest protection, and local control.
