## METACOUPLING FRAMEWORK OVERVIEW

The metacoupling framework (Liu, 2017) is an integrated framework for understanding human-nature interactions across all spatial scales. It encompasses three types of couplings:

- **Intracoupling**: Human-nature interactions within a coupled human and natural system (Liu, 2017). The intracoupling framework focuses on interactions between human and nature subsystems within one focal system, through flows facilitated by agents, generated by causes, and leading to effects within both the focal system and any spillover systems.

- **Pericoupling**: Human-nature interactions between adjacent coupled human and natural systems (Liu, 2017). The pericoupling framework has the same structure as the telecoupling framework (systems, flows, agents, causes, effects) except that sending and receiving systems are nearby rather than far away. For example, flows of labor and materials between neighboring counties or nations.

- **Telecoupling**: Human-nature interactions between distant coupled human and natural systems (Liu et al., 2013). Telecoupling involves sending, receiving, and spillover systems connected by flows of information, energy, material, people, organisms, and capital across large geographic distances. Research shows that in many cases, telecouplings are stronger than pericouplings, challenging Tobler's First Law of Geography (Liu, 2023).


A coupled human and natural system (CHANS) contains interconnected human (socioeconomic) and natural (environmental/ecological) components that interact with each other.


## FIVE FRAMEWORK COMPONENTS

The telecoupling framework (Liu et al., 2013) identifies five interrelated components for analyzing distant human-nature interactions:


### Systems

Coupled human and natural systems involved in the interaction. Each system contains both a human (socioeconomic) subsystem and a natural (environmental/ecological) subsystem that interact with each other. Systems are classified by their role: sending, receiving, or spillover.


Sub-elements:
  - Sending system(s) — origin of flows
  - Receiving system(s) — destination of flows
  - Spillover system(s) — indirectly affected systems
  - Human subsystem within each system (governance, economy, society)
  - Natural subsystem within each system (ecosystems, biodiversity, climate)

Guiding questions:
  - What are the coupled human and natural systems involved?
  - Which system(s) send flows and which receive them?
  - Are there spillover systems affected indirectly?
  - What are the human and natural subsystems within each system?
  - Can system roles change over time or be bidirectional?


### Flows

Movements of matter, energy, information, capital, organisms, or people between systems (Liu, 2017, Table 1). Flows are what connect the coupled systems and can be unidirectional or bidirectional.


Sub-elements:
  - Capital flows (investment, aid, payments, remittances, trade revenue)
  - Energy flows (electricity, embodied energy, fossil fuels)
  - Information flows (knowledge, technology, data, cultural practices)
  - Matter flows (goods, commodities, water, waste, natural resources)
  - Organism flows (species invasion, seed dispersal, animal migration)
  - People flows (migration, tourism, labor movement, refugees)

Guiding questions:
  - What is being transferred between systems?
  - What categories do the flows belong to (capital, energy, information, matter, organisms, people)?
  - What is the direction and magnitude of each flow?
  - Are flows unidirectional or bidirectional?
  - How have flows changed over time?


### Agents

Autonomous decision-making entities that directly or indirectly facilitate or hinder coupling processes. Agents are grouped into five fixed categories: individuals / households; firms / traders / corporations; governments / policymakers; organizations / NGOs; and non-human agents.


Sub-elements:
  - Individuals / households (farmers, consumers, tourists, migrants, fishers, local residents)
  - Firms / traders / corporations (agribusinesses, exporters, importers, logistics companies)
  - Governments / policymakers (public agencies, regulators, elected officials, planning bodies)
  - Organizations / NGOs (NGOs, universities, certification bodies, international organizations)
  - Non-human agents (migratory animals, invasive species, pathogens, pests, crops, livestock)
  - Agents in sending systems
  - Agents in receiving systems
  - Agents facilitating the coupling (brokers, intermediaries)

Guiding questions:
  - Who or what drives the interactions between systems?
  - What agents exist in the sending, receiving, and spillover systems?
  - Which fixed agent category does each agent belong to?
  - What are the agents' motivations and decision-making processes?
  - How do different agent categories interact?
  - Are there intermediary agents facilitating the telecoupling?
  - Are any non-human entities active agents rather than only flows or affected resources?


### Causes

Factors that drive coupling processes, grouped into the fixed MetacouplingAssistant cause/effect categories.


Sub-elements:
  - Economic causes (markets, prices, demand, investment, costs)
  - Political / institutional causes (policies, governance, regulation, institutions)
  - Ecological / biological causes (ecosystem processes, species, habitat, pests)
  - Technological / infrastructural causes (technology, roads, ports, logistics, dams)
  - Cultural / social / demographic causes (values, diets, migration, population change)
  - Hydrological causes (water availability, water quality, drought, flooding)
  - Climatic / atmospheric causes (climate variability, emissions, air transport)
  - Geological / geomorphological causes (soils, terrain, erosion, deposits)

Guiding questions:
  - What economic forces drive the coupling?
  - What political or institutional conditions drive the coupling?
  - What ecological or biological processes contribute to the interaction?
  - What technological or infrastructural factors enable or constrain it?
  - What cultural, social, or demographic factors are involved?
  - What hydrological conditions play a role?
  - What climatic or atmospheric conditions play a role?
  - What geological or geomorphological conditions are involved?
  - How do causes differ between sending and receiving systems?
  - Are there external/global causes (e.g., international policy, climate change)?


### Effects

Consequences and outcomes of coupling processes on coupled human and natural systems, grouped into the fixed MetacouplingAssistant cause/effect categories. Effects can be positive, negative, or mixed for each system.


Sub-elements:
  - Economic effects (income, employment, costs, market shifts)
  - Political / institutional effects (governance, regulation, enforcement)
  - Ecological / biological effects (biodiversity, habitat, species, ecosystem services)
  - Technological / infrastructural effects (infrastructure, innovation, logistics)
  - Cultural / social / demographic effects (well-being, migration, equity, norms)
  - Hydrological effects (water quantity, quality, runoff, watershed change)
  - Climatic / atmospheric effects (GHG emissions, air pollution, climate feedbacks)
  - Geological / geomorphological effects (soil change, erosion, sedimentation)
  - Synergies and tradeoffs between effects
  - Feedback effects (how effects alter causes or flows)

Guiding questions:
  - Which fixed effect categories are relevant for each system?
  - Are effects positive, negative, or mixed for each system?
  - How do effects on sending vs. receiving systems compare?
  - What spillover effects occur on third-party systems?
  - Are there feedback loops where effects alter causes or flows?
  - What synergies or tradeoffs exist among the effects?


## TELECOUPLING CATEGORIES

Liu et al. (2013) identified 14 major categories of telecoupling. A single study may involve one or more of these categories:

1. **Trade**: International or long-distance exchange of goods and commodities that connects distant production and consumption systems.
   Example: Brazil-to-China soybean trade driving deforestation and land-use change

2. **Development investment**: Foreign direct investment and development projects that connect investor and host country systems.
   Example: Chinese investment in African agricultural and mining projects

3. **Transnational land transfer**: Large-scale acquisition or lease of land in one country by entities from another country (land grabbing).
   Example: Saudi Arabia leasing farmland in Ethiopia for food production

4. **Conservation investment**: Financial and institutional flows for biodiversity conservation and environmental protection across distant systems.
   Example: International funding for tropical rainforest conservation

5. **Technology transfer**: Movement of technology, equipment, and technical capacity between distant systems.
   Example: Transfer of renewable energy technology from developed to developing nations

6. **Knowledge transfer**: Exchange of scientific knowledge, educational resources, and expertise between distant systems.
   Example: International research collaborations on climate change adaptation

7. **Human migration**: Movement of people between distant systems for economic, political, or environmental reasons.
   Example: Rural-to-urban migration from agricultural regions to industrial cities

8. **Tourism**: Travel of people between distant systems for recreation, creating socioeconomic and environmental impacts in both origin and destination systems.
   Example: International ecotourism to panda reserves in China

9. **Waste transfer**: Movement of waste, pollutants, or discarded materials between distant systems.
   Example: International e-waste shipments from developed to developing countries

10. **Species invasion**: Introduction and spread of non-native species between distant systems, often facilitated by trade or transport.
   Example: Red imported fire ant invasion from USA to China via shipping containers

11. **Animal migration**: Natural seasonal or life-cycle movement of animal species between distant systems.
   Example: Migratory bird flyways connecting breeding and wintering habitats

12. **Water transfer**: Engineered or natural movement of water resources between distant systems, including inter-basin transfers and virtual water.
   Example: South-to-North Water Transfer Project in China supplying Beijing

13. **Species dispersal**: Natural spread and distribution of species between distant systems through ecological processes.
   Example: Seed dispersal by migratory animals connecting forest ecosystems

14. **Atmospheric circulation**: Large-scale movement of atmospheric components (dust, pollutants, moisture) between distant systems.
   Example: Saharan dust transport to the Amazon Basin fertilizing soils


## KEY ANALYTICAL INSIGHTS

### Operationalization Procedure (Liu, 2017)

Operationalizing the metacoupling framework involves six phases:
  1. Set research goals — formulate objectives, questions, hypotheses
  2. Define focal system(s) — determined by research goals and feasibility
  3. Review literature / conduct studies on flows, agents, causes, effects — trace flows across space to determine system boundaries
  4. Finalize system boundaries — where flows start and end define boundaries; entities affecting flows are agents; reasons for flows are causes
  5. Collect and analyze data — quantitative and qualitative
  6. Communicate results — publications, stakeholder engagement

### Coupling Transformations (Liu, 2023)

Systems can undergo four stages of transformation:
  - Noncoupling: no human-nature interactions within or between systems
  - Coupling: interactions emerge among components
  - Decoupling: interactions weaken or dissolve (e.g., due to shocks like economic recession, pandemic, or war)
  - Recoupling: weakened or dissolved interactions are recovered
These stages can occur in intracoupling, pericoupling, and telecoupling simultaneously or separately.

### Complex Interactions Among Coupling Types (Liu, 2023)

  - Intracoupling, pericoupling, and telecoupling can have synergistic effects
  - Pericoupling and telecoupling can amplify intracoupling
  - Increases in one type of coupling may reduce other types
  - One type of coupling may generate benefits on one scale at the cost of other scales (spatial tradeoffs)
  - Telecoupling, pericoupling, and intracoupling can interact indirectly
  - Feedbacks (positive or negative) are common and may take time to emerge

### Cascading Interactions (Liu, 2023)

An event in one place can cascade and spread impacts on other places both nearby and far away through multiple steps. For example, drought in the US Midwest reduced soybean production, potentially increasing global soybean prices, which could encourage deforestation in Brazil for expanded soybean cultivation.

### Flow-Based Governance (Liu, 2023)

The framework supports shifting from place-based governance (focus on specific places independently) to flow-based governance (focus on movements of people, information, energy, matter, organisms, goods, capital among places). This shift can enhance global sustainability and improve spatial justice and equity.


## ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY

Organize the analysis around the **coupling type** as the primary axis. The metacoupling framework distinguishes three coupling types — intracoupling (within the focal system), pericoupling (adjacent systems), and telecoupling (distant systems) — and the analysis should mirror that distinction at the top level. Within each coupling type, walk through the framework components (systems, flows, agents, causes, effects).

Follow this systematic approach based on the six-phase operationalization procedure (Liu, 2017):

1. **Read and understand** the research description carefully, identifying the core research question and study context (Phase 1: Set research goals).

2. **Define the focal system(s)** (Phase 2): Identify the primary coupled human and natural system(s) that the research focuses on.

3. **Classify the coupling types present** in this study. Determine which of the three coupling types apply:
   - **Intracoupling** (always present): interactions WITHIN the focal system.
   - **Pericoupling** (often present): interactions with geographically adjacent systems at any spatial scale. In this package the most common cases are neighboring nations and cross-border ADM1 regions (states / provinces); in principle any pair of adjacent units qualifies (e.g., neighboring counties, municipalities, or ecosystems), though country and ADM1 are what the bundled databases and map renderers support.
   - **Telecoupling** (often present): interactions with geographically distant systems (international trade partners, non-adjacent regions).

   A study often involves multiple types simultaneously. Intracoupling, pericoupling, and telecoupling can have synergistic effects, and increases in one type may reduce others.

4. **For EACH active coupling type, conduct a complete component analysis** with the following sub-steps. Repeat the entire sub-step block once per active coupling type:

   a. **Identify systems** (Phase 3-4): Determine the relevant systems for this coupling type. For intracoupling, that is the focal system only. For pericoupling, that is the focal system plus each adjacent system. For telecoupling, classify each system as sending, receiving, or spillover. For EVERY system, describe the human subsystem, natural subsystem, and geographic scope. Trace flows across space — where flows start and end define system boundaries. Pay special attention to **spillover** systems in telecoupling, which are often overlooked but can experience effects larger than those in sending and receiving systems (Liu, 2023). Spillover systems must be analyzed with the same rigor as sending and receiving systems.

   b. **Map flows**: Identify what moves between systems within this coupling type — matter (goods, water, waste), energy, information, capital (money, investment), organisms, or people. Specify direction and categorize each flow using one of the six categories (Liu, 2017, Table 1). Consider both direct flows and virtual/embodied flows (e.g., virtual water, embodied energy in traded goods). **When a flow operates at multiple scales** (e.g., an international payment that also drives in-state investment), include it under EVERY applicable coupling-type section — duplication is intentional and expected so each section reads as a complete picture of that scale.

   c. **Identify agents** active in this coupling type: who or what drives the interactions using the fixed agent categories: individuals / households; firms / traders / corporations; governments / policymakers; organizations / NGOs; and non-human agents. Consider small agents (e.g., smallholder farmers) whose agency can be enhanced through metacoupling processes (Liu, 2023).

   d. **Analyze causes** for this coupling type, grouped by the fixed MetacouplingAssistant categories: economic; political / institutional; ecological / biological; technological / infrastructural; cultural / social / demographic; hydrological; climatic / atmospheric; geological / geomorphological. Include only relevant categories.

   e. **Assess effects** for this coupling type, grouped by the same fixed categories used for causes: economic; political / institutional; ecological / biological; technological / infrastructural; cultural / social / demographic; hydrological; climatic / atmospheric; geological / geomorphological - noting both positive and negative consequences, synergies, and tradeoffs. For each effect, indicate which system within this coupling type it applies to. Include only relevant categories.

5. **Analyze cross-coupling interactions**: After completing the per-type analyses, examine how the active coupling types interact:
   - Do they amplify or offset each other?
   - Are there spatial tradeoffs where one scale benefits at the cost of another?
   - Feedback effects (positive or negative) that may take time to emerge.
   - Cascading interactions across space (an event in one system triggering chain reactions in distant systems).
   - Coupling transformations (noncoupling -> coupling -> decoupling -> recoupling) under global shocks.

6. **Identify research gaps**: Suggest what additional data, analyses, or perspectives would strengthen the analysis. Consider flow-based governance implications (Liu, 2023).

Important: If the research description is vague or incomplete, ask clarifying questions rather than making assumptions. If a coupling type is not present in this study, OMIT its top-level section entirely from the output — do not leave a placeholder header.

### Soybean, Corn and Wheat Trade: Brazil, China and the Mercosur Neighbours
Source: Herzberger, A., Chung, M. G., Kapsar, K., Frank, K. A., & Liu, J. (2019). Telecoupled food trade affects pericoupled trade and intracoupled production. Sustainability, 11(10), 2908.
Adaptation: The paper names no spillover system; the United States, whose share of China's soybean imports Brazil overtook, is shown as one, as in Liu et al. (2013). The paper also counts Paraguay-Uruguay trade as pericoupled; the two countries share no border, so that flow is left out.

Herzberger et al. (2019) ask how a distant flow, Brazil's soybean exports to China, changed trade between adjacent countries and production within them. Using FAOSTAT production and UN Comtrade trade data for soybeans, corn and wheat in 1991-2016, they compare the years before and after China cut its soybean import tariff from 130% to 3% in 1995. Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay were studied because each shares a border and Mercosur membership with Brazil.

**Coupling Classification**
- **Intracoupling**: Soybean, corn and wheat production within each country, such as Brazil's expansion as farmers gained access to imported fertilizers, pesticides and seeds.
- **Pericoupling**: Crop trade and investment among Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay, countries that share land borders.
- **Telecoupling**: Soybean exports from Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay to China, and from Paraguay to Russia and Turkey, none of which share a border with the exporter.

**Intracoupling (within the focal system)**
*Systems*
**Focal System**: Brazil
- **Human subsystem**: Soybean, corn and wheat farmers; a government that liberalised trade and farmers' access to inputs
- **Natural subsystem**: Cropland for soybean, corn and wheat, including land in the Amazon and Cerrado biomes
- **Geographic scope**: Brazil
*Flows*
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Brazilian farms → domestic markets and export ports
- **Description**: Soybeans, corn and wheat produced within Brazil
*Agents*
- [Individuals / households] Brazilian farmers: Expanded soybean production with imported fertilizers, pesticides and seeds
- [Governments / policymakers] Brazilian government: Liberalised trade and farmers' access to inputs
*Causes*
**Political / Institutional**
- Brazil liberalised trade and farmers' access to fertilizers, pesticides and seeds
*Effects*
**Economic**
- Brazil emerged as the most competitive soybean exporter and passed the United States in production in 2013 (applies to: Brazil, focal system)
**Ecological / Biological**
- Citing Dou et al., the paper notes that conservation agreements such as the Soy Moratorium shifted deforestation from the Amazon to the Cerrado (applies to: Brazil, focal system)

**Pericoupling (adjacent systems)**
*Systems*
**Sending System (adjacent)**: Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay (for crop exports to Brazil)
- **Human subsystem**: Farmers and exporters; Argentina's government, which taxes soybean exports outside Mercosur
- **Natural subsystem**: Cropland for soybean, corn and wheat
- **Geographic scope**: The three Mercosur members that border Brazil
**Receiving System (adjacent)**: Brazil (for crops from its neighbours)
- **Human subsystem**: Importers and processors of neighbours' soybean, corn and wheat
- **Natural subsystem**: Brazilian cropland, whose output these imports supplement
- **Geographic scope**: Brazil
**Receiving System (adjacent)**: Uruguay (for Argentine investment)
- **Human subsystem**: A soybean sector expanding with investment from Argentine producers
- **Natural subsystem**: Farmland about 50% cheaper than soybean land in Argentina
- **Geographic scope**: Uruguay
*Flows*
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Paraguay → Brazil
- **Description**: Soybeans, corn and wheat; compared with 1991-1995, these exports rose by 114%, 1,712% and 6,474%
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Argentina → Brazil
- **Description**: Soybeans, corn and wheat; soybean exports stopped in 1995 and resumed consistently only around 2005
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Uruguay → Brazil
- **Description**: Soybeans and corn; soybean exports fell to zero in 1997 and did not resume consistently
**Capital Flow**
- **Direction**: Argentina → Uruguay
- **Description**: Investment by Argentine producers, the main driver of Uruguay's soybean expansion
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Paraguay → Argentina
- **Description**: Soybeans shipped by barge to Argentine river ports
*Agents*
- [Governments / policymakers] Argentine government: Imposed a 23.5% soybean export tax in 2006 that exempts Mercosur partners
- [Firms / traders / corporations] Argentine producers: Invested in Uruguayan soybean farming
- [Governments / policymakers] Paraguayan government: Keeps diplomatic ties with Taiwan, which closes the Chinese market to Paraguay
*Causes*
**Economic**
- Uruguayan land costs about half as much as Argentine soybean land, and an early-2000s financial crisis encouraged investment
**Political / Institutional**
- Shared borders and joint Mercosur membership make neighbour trade feasible, and Argentina's export tax does not apply to Mercosur buyers
**Technological / Infrastructural**
- Paraguay ships its soybeans by barge to river ports in neighbouring countries
*Effects*
**Economic**
- After the 2006 export tax, soybean area grew 45% (2006-2016, against 215% in 1995-2005), corn area grew faster, and soybean exports shifted toward Mercosur neighbours (applies to: Argentina, sending system)
- Shut out of China, Paraguay increased exports of all three crops to Brazil (applies to: Paraguay, sending system)

**Telecoupling (distant systems)**
*Systems*
**Sending System (distant)**: Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay (for soybean exports to China)
- **Human subsystem**: Soybean exporters and the governments that set export rules
- **Natural subsystem**: Soybean cropland
- **Geographic scope**: The three exporters
**Receiving System (distant)**: China
- **Human subsystem**: A growing middle class eating more meat; importers after the 1995 tariff cut
- **Natural subsystem**: Not analysed in the paper
- **Geographic scope**: China
**Receiving System (distant)**: Russia and Turkey (for Paraguayan soybeans)
- **Human subsystem**: Processors that export soybean oil to China
- **Natural subsystem**: Not analysed in the paper
- **Geographic scope**: Russia and Turkey
**Spillover System**: United States
- **Human subsystem**: Soybean exporters whose share of China's imports fell from 68% in 1995 to 40% in 2016
- **Natural subsystem**: US soybean cropland (not analysed in the paper)
- **Geographic scope**: The United States
*Flows*
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Brazil → China
- **Description**: Soybeans; Brazil's share of China's soybean imports rose from 0% in 1991-1995 to 46% in 2016
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Argentina and Uruguay → China
- **Description**: Soybeans; Uruguay's exports to China rose 1,250% from 2005 to 2016, while Argentina's grew only 23% after its 2006 export tax
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Paraguay → Russia and Turkey
- **Description**: Soybeans, which those countries process and export to China as soybean oil
**Information Flow**
- **Direction**: China → the world soybean market
- **Description**: The demand signal of China's 1995 soybean tariff cut from 130% to 3%
*Agents*
- [Governments / policymakers] Chinese government: Cut the soybean import tariff in 1995
- [Individuals / households] China's middle class: Eats more meat, the main driver of China's soybean demand
- [Firms / traders / corporations] Multinational companies: Invested in Uruguay's soybean expansion
*Causes*
**Political / Institutional**
- China's 1995 tariff cut; European and US biofuel mandates, which drew Argentine land into corn
**Cultural / Social / Demographic**
- Rising meat consumption by China's middle class
*Effects*
**Economic**
- Brazil passed the United States in China's soybean market in 2006 (applies to: United States, spillover system)

**Cross-coupling Interactions**
- **Amplification and offset of pericoupled trade**: Brazil's soybean exports to China were significantly related to its soybean imports from its neighbours. Pericoupled trade was enhanced for some partners and crops, such as Paraguay's exports of all three crops to Brazil, and offset for others, such as Uruguay's soybean and corn exports to Brazil, which fell to zero or became intermittent after China's 1995 tariff cut.
- **Displacement of trade toward neighbours**: Argentina's 2006 export tax slowed its soybean exports to China, which rose 23% afterwards, while exports to Mercosur neighbours, exempt from the tax, grew faster.

### Wolong Nature Reserve, China: Panda Loans, Tourism and Marriage Migration
Source: Liu, J. (2017). Integration across a metacoupled world. Ecology and Society, 22(4), 29.

Liu (2017) illustrates the metacoupling framework with Wolong Nature Reserve, a 2,000 km² reserve in Sichuan, China, home to giant pandas and about 5,000 residents in more than 1,100 households. Two processes stand for each coupling type: fuelwood and timber collection within the reserve; wild-panda movement and marriage migration with the bordering counties; and panda loans and tourism with distant places. Wolong sends some flows and receives others, so its role is given per flow.

**Coupling Classification**
- **Intracoupling**: Residents collect fuelwood and timber from the reserve's forests and panda habitat, and forest-protection programs pay households to restore and guard the forest.
- **Pericoupling**: Wild pandas cross the reserve boundary into abutting areas, and women from the bordering counties marry into Wolong households; the paper treats systems that share a boundary as adjacent.
- **Telecoupling**: Captive pandas are loaned to zoos around the world, and tourists arrive from across China and other countries, places that share no boundary with Wolong.

**Intracoupling (within the focal system)**
*Systems*
**Focal System**: Wolong Nature Reserve, Sichuan Province, China
- **Human subsystem**: About 5,000 residents, mostly farmers, in more than 1,100 households; reserve managers and policy makers
- **Natural subsystem**: Forests, giant pandas and their habitat, and over 6,000 other animal and plant species
- **Geographic scope**: The 2,000 km² reserve in southwestern China
*Flows*
**People Flow**
- **Direction**: Households → forests and panda habitat
- **Description**: Residents going into the forest to collect wood and carry out other activities
**Matter Flow**
- **Direction**: Forests and panda habitat → households
- **Description**: Fuelwood, timber and non-timber products for cooking, heating and building
**Information Flow**
- **Direction**: Forests and pandas → policy makers
- **Description**: Information on the condition of forests, pandas and their habitat
*Agents*
- [Individuals / households] Wolong households: Collect fuelwood and timber and farm inside the reserve
- [Governments / policymakers] Reserve managers and policy makers: Run the forest-protection programs and use information on forest and panda conditions
*Causes*
**Economic**
- Households need fuelwood for cooking and heating
**Political / Institutional**
- The Grain-to-Green Program (2000) pays farmers to return steep cropland to forest, and the Natural Forest Conservation Program (2001) bans logging and pays households to monitor forests
*Effects*
**Ecological / Biological**
- Wood collection degraded and fragmented panda habitat; the forest programs work to restore it (applies to: Wolong, focal system)
**Climatic / Atmospheric**
- CO2 from burning fuelwood leaves the reserve; a small hydropower station reduces fuelwood use (applies to: the rest of the world, spillover system)

**Pericoupling (adjacent systems)**
*Systems*
**Sending System (adjacent)**: Xiaojin, Li and Wenchuan counties (for marriage migration)
- **Human subsystem**: Families whose daughters marry into Wolong; matchmakers and hometown networks
- **Natural subsystem**: Rural mountain landscapes outside the reserve (not analysed in the paper)
- **Geographic scope**: Counties that share a boundary with the reserve
**Receiving System (adjacent)**: Wolong Nature Reserve (for marriage migration)
- **Human subsystem**: Households gaining brides, and with them more people and households
- **Natural subsystem**: Forests facing higher demand for natural resources
- **Geographic scope**: The reserve
**Sending System (adjacent)**: Wolong Nature Reserve (for wild-panda movement)
- **Human subsystem**: Wolong Administration Bureau, which protects panda habitat and movement corridors
- **Natural subsystem**: Wild giant pandas and their habitat inside the reserve
- **Geographic scope**: The reserve
**Receiving System (adjacent)**: Areas abutting the reserve (for wild-panda movement)
- **Human subsystem**: Land users and agencies next to the boundary (not detailed in the paper)
- **Natural subsystem**: Panda habitat beyond an unfenced boundary
- **Geographic scope**: Land next to the reserve boundary
**Spillover System**: Other neighbouring counties and areas affected by immigrants or moving pandas
- **Human subsystem**: Communities whose residents leave or arrive through marriage
- **Natural subsystem**: Habitat affected by panda movement beyond the abutting areas
- **Geographic scope**: The wider region around Wolong
*Flows*
**People Flow**
- **Direction**: Xiaojin, Li and Wenchuan counties → Wolong
- **Description**: Women marrying into Wolong; in 2010, 49 of 370 married women in 287 interviewed households were born in neighbouring counties
**Organisms Flow**
- **Direction**: Wolong → areas abutting the reserve

- **Indonesia:** Land-rights disputes and decent-work risks can accompany economic gains. The ILO identifies safety, wages, working hours, discrimination, and organizing rights as important concerns, without estimating their national prevalence. [T1:W14] [T1:W15]

**Hydrological**
- **Indonesia:** Conversion can change runoff and nutrient loading. In the **Kais watershed model**, runoff increased by 21%, total nitrogen by 78%, and total phosphorus by 144%. These are catchment-specific modeled changes, not national averages. [T1:W9]

**Climatic / Atmospheric**
- **Indonesia:** Forest conversion, peat drainage, fire, and mill-effluent methane create emissions. [T1:W6] [T1:W8]
- **Indonesia:** Biodiesel is not automatically climate beneficial. A Jambi-based life-cycle study found no greenhouse-gas savings for first-rotation plantations established following forest clearance under the scenarios examined. [T1:W7]

**Geological / Geomorphological**
- **Indonesia:** Peat subsidence and sediment production can impose persistent costs. Evidence includes the West Kalimantan case investigation and modeled sediment increases in Kais; neither establishes a uniform national effect. [T1:W14] [T1:W9]

## 5. Cross-coupling Interactions

- **Amplification / offset:** Adjacent and distant demand can amplify domestic plantation and processing activity, while domestic biodiesel demand can compete with exports for feedstock. Export levies also finance domestic biodiesel subsidies, directly connecting cross-border capital with within-country incentives. However, domestic consumption and exports both increased in the supplied 2025 statistics, so greater domestic use should not automatically be interpreted as reducing exports. [T1:W3] [T1:W20]

- **Spatial tradeoffs:** Importing systems obtain palm products while many forest, water, and land-conversion burdens occur in Indonesian producing landscapes. Within Indonesia, benefits and costs also differ geographically and socially: market-connected communities and remote forest-dependent communities have experienced contrasting welfare outcomes. Aggregate export earnings therefore cannot establish equitable sustainability. [T1:1] [T1:W13]

- **Spillover effects:** International certification can influence clearing both inside certified supply bases and elsewhere in Indonesian landscapes. A review reports positive and negative effects around certified operations in Kalimantan, with an insignificant overall net effect in the summarized analysis. At the national boundary used here, these are within-Indonesia consequences associated with an internationally connected intervention—not proof that reductions inside certified estates equal reductions across the wider landscape. [T1:5]

## 6. Research Gaps and Suggestions

1. **Specify the assessment period and sustainability priorities.** Which years, provinces, and outcomes matter most—household welfare, worker rights, biodiversity, water, climate, or their combination? A national assessment should retain regional and producer-type differences.

2. **Trace production to domestic and export destinations.** Link plantations → mills → processors → traders → destinations, including domestic biodiesel. Separate product categories and identify whether Malaysian purchases are consumed, processed, or re-exported. Official BPS production and trade tables provide a potential baseline, but do not alone establish plantation-to-destination links. [T1:W2]

3. **Strengthen causal attribution and account for delayed conversion.** Distinguish effects of export demand from domestic mandates, prices, infrastructure, and land institutions. Reconstruct land histories: land appearing unused before planting may have been cleared years earlier. [T1:W5]

4. **Measure distribution, not just sector totals.** Collect household income and cost data, wages, occupational risks, tenure histories, compensation, and access to remedies. Disaggregate independent smallholders, scheme participants, workers, and forest-dependent residents. [T1:W13] [T1:W14] [T1:W15]

5. **Validate environmental attribution and candidate spillovers.** Combine forest histories, peat condition, mill emissions, and watershed monitoring with sourcing data. Test Malaysian competitive responses and any transboundary pollution pathways before classifying them as observed effects. Do not attribute Indonesia’s entire fire burden to palm oil; the World Bank explicitly cautions against that attribution. [T1:W8] [T1:W9] [T1:W10]

6. **Evaluate flow-based governance across domestic and foreign markets.** Test coordinated traceability, smallholder support, worker participation, public land information, and financing arrangements—not export standards alone. Include effects beyond participating suppliers and whether domestic-market demand sustains pressures that foreign requirements seek to reduce. [T1:5] [T1:W15] [T1:W18]

## 7. Evidence Coverage

**Strong multi-source support:** The connection between oil-palm expansion and forest, peat, biodiversity, and emissions impacts is supported across the retrieved study and several environmental studies. Evidence also supports heterogeneous social outcomes rather than uniform poverty reduction or uniform harm. [T1:1] [T1:W4] [T1:W6] [T1:W7] [T1:W13]

**Limited or scale-specific evidence:** The quoted 2025 market totals come from an industry association, not an independent sustainability evaluation. Kais results are modeled catchment outcomes, Jambi biodiesel findings concern particular production conditions, and the certification estimates cover historical periods. These findings should not be combined as interchangeable national or current-year measurements. [T1:W3] [T1:W9] [T1:W7] [T1:W16]

**Inferred without direct measurement:** Bilateral financial distributions, environmental changes within importing countries, destination-specific deforestation footprints, and Malaysia’s competitive response remain unquantified. Malaysia is therefore a **candidate**, not a confirmed spillover system. The preparedness exercise establishes shared sourcing and implementation challenges, not displaced production or forest loss. Some retrieved passages are also summarized or truncated, limiting detailed verification. [T1:W24]
